Genotype-by-environment scans: loci acting differently in each sex

Do loci affect growth differently in males and females? This vignette treats sex as the environment: it estimates the genetic covariance of growth within and between the sexes, and scans for genotype-by-sex interactions with two backgrounds. It builds on the vignettes Estimating G for many traits and Genome scans for many traits, and was precomputed.

Two features here are experimental. fit_G_env() estimates the genetic covariance between environments, and in simulations it estimated cross-environment genetic correlations too low (0.67-0.77 where the truth was 1). Scans that use it are conservative for interactions: they do not report too many interactions, but they miss some.

Genetic covariance within and between the sexes

fit_G_env() treats each trait in each sex as its own trait (a “character state”): growth12:F is observed in females, growth12:M in males. Its G holds the genetic covariance within each sex and between the sexes. The environment’s main effect is in the states’ own means, so the sex column of X is left out:

fit_env <- fit_G_env(Y, env = d$SEX, X = X[, colnames(X) != "SEXM"], A = A,
                     cores = cores, seed = 1L, progress = FALSE)

corr_between is each trait’s genetic correlation between the sexes. A value of 1 means the same genes act in both sexes in the same way; below 1, the genetic effects differ between the sexes: genotype-by-sex interaction in the polygenic background. Here they run from 0.35 to 0.95. Given the downward bias above, values somewhat below 1 are not by themselves evidence of interaction; the lowest ones are the most suggestive.

Why the background matters for an interaction scan

An interaction scan asks, for each marker, whether its effect differs between the sexes. If the polygenic background has genotype-by-sex interaction and the scan’s covariance ignores it, markers that track the relatedness of the mice pick some of it up, and the interaction test reports too many hits. In simulations (STATUS.md) a cross-sex genetic correlation of 0.5 raised the genomic inflation of the interaction test to about 1.14; scanning with the environment-specific covariance from fit_G_env() brought it to 0.94-0.98.

Two scans

The usual background (G and E from fit_G()) and the environment-specific one, both with gxe = "SEXM": each marker’s dosage also enters multiplied by the male indicator.

usual <- indri_gwas(Y, dosage, fit = fit, A = A, X = X, snp_info = info,
                    gxe = "SEXM")
time_env <- system.time(
  by_sex <- indri_gwas(Y, dosage, fit = fit_env, A = A, X = X,
                       snp_info = info, gxe = "SEXM"))
lambda <- function(p, df) qchisq(median(p, na.rm = TRUE), df, lower.tail = FALSE) /
  qchisq(0.5, df, lower.tail = FALSE)
data.frame(background = c("usual", "environment-specific"),
           lambda_interaction = round(c(lambda(usual$pvalues_gxe, ncol(Y)),
                                        lambda(by_sex$pvalues_gxe, ncol(Y))), 3),
           delta = round(c(usual$delta, by_sex$delta), 2))

pvalues_gxe tests the interaction given the marker’s main effect, with nine degrees of freedom (one per trait); pvalues is the joint test of the main effect and the interaction.

threshold <- 0.05 / ncol(dosage)

The interaction tests are inflated with either background (genomic inflation 1.56 with the usual one, 1.29 with the environment-specific one), and the environment-specific background removes part of it, as in the simulations. What remains may be genotype-by-sex interaction spread over many linked loci, or variance the model does not have, such as litter effects that differ between the sexes. No marker passes the Bonferroni threshold with either background (0 and 0): these data give no strong evidence of single loci acting differently in males and females.

Sex-specific effects

a is a marker’s effect where the environment column is 0 (females), and a + gxe its effect in males. At the marker with the strongest interaction, which is not significant, the two profiles show what an interaction would look like:

j <- which.min(by_sex$pvalues_gxe)
effects <- rbind(
  data.frame(sex = "female", week = seq_along(traits), effect = by_sex$a[j, ]),
  data.frame(sex = "male", week = seq_along(traits),
             effect = by_sex$a[j, ] + by_sex$gxe[j, , "SEXM"]))
library(ggplot2)
ggplot(effects, aes(week, effect, colour = sex)) +
  geom_hline(yintercept = 0, colour = "grey60") +
  geom_line() + geom_point() +
  labs(title = paste0(info$snp[j], " (chromosome ", info$chrom[j], ")"),
       x = "growth interval (week k to k + 1)",
       y = "additive effect (grams)") +
  theme_minimal()

Cost

The environment-specific covariance is not a Kronecker product, so the scan cannot use the single rotation of the usual one. It rotates by a generalized eigendecomposition once per group of related mice instead, which keeps it fast for family data like these (seconds here) and makes it expensive for a dense genomic relationship matrix.

Summary

  • fit_G_env() estimates genetic covariance within and between the sexes; its cross-sex correlations are currently biased low.
  • Interaction scans need a background that allows genotype-by-sex interaction, or they report too many interactions; with fit_G_env()’s covariance they are conservative instead.
  • Dominance enters such scans as a main effect only.
