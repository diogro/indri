Estimating G for many traits: engines and priors

This vignette estimates the additive genetic covariance matrix G of nine growth traits in mice with each of indri’s three engines, shows how to read a fit, and gives a rule for choosing the prior of the Bayesian engine. It was precomputed: the fits take from seconds (REML) to many minutes (stanimal).

How indri estimates G

A multivariate animal model on many traits is expensive: the number of parameters grows with the square of the number of traits, and fitting all of them at once is slow and often unstable. indri, following LiMMBo (Meyer et al. 2018), fits many small animal models instead, each on a subset of the traits, so that every pair of traits is estimated in several subsets. It then averages the subset estimates of each covariance and projects the result onto the positive semi-definite matrices, so that the assembled G is a covariance matrix. The small fit is done by a pluggable engine: "sommer" and "asreml" (REML) or "stanimal" (a Bayesian sampler).

The data

An F3 intercross of two inbred mouse strains selected for large (LG/J) and small (SM/J) body size ships with indri. The parental strains are fully inbred and the F1 all heterozygous, so the F2 parents of the F3 are unrelated founders, and the F3 mice are related only through them: full sibs (1/2) and, for nine sires with two mates, half sibs (1/4). lgsm_A is that relationship matrix. The traits are the nine weekly weight gains (growth12, the gain from week 1 to week 2, to growth910), and the fixed effects those of the original analyses: sex, rearing cohort and litter size at birth and at weaning.

One caveat before any fit: the full sibs also share a mother and a litter, and indri’s models have one random effect, the additive one. Variance shared by littermates (maternal effects, a common nest) therefore ends up partly in G. Some pups were cross-fostered, which separates the litter environment from the mother, but the model here does not use it. Read the G below as an additive-plus-litter covariance; the original analyses included a dam effect.

The stanimal engine samples each block’s posterior with CmdStan. It is far slower, and gives posterior draws instead of a normal approximation. Each block runs four chains here, so a budget of cores processes runs cores / 4 blocks at a time:

The two REML engines fit the same model by the same criterion and agree almost exactly (their genetic correlations correlate at 0.999). stanimal’s agree closely too (0.99), at 18 minutes against seconds: it fits each pair in three blocks (33 here, against 16 for REML) and samples each with four chains. Its correlations are a little closer to zero, the pull of the prior, which the next section examines.

Why use stanimal, given the time? It brings four things the REML engines do not:

  • A posterior, not a normal approximation. The REML engines’ intervals come from a normal approximation to each block’s sampling distribution, which fits poorly near the boundaries (a variance near zero, a correlation near ±1); stanimal’s draws respect them, so every draw of a correlation is a correlation.
  • Tighter intervals at the same coverage. In simulations of 35 traits on 300 individuals (STATUS.md), stanimal’s 95% intervals for the genetic correlations covered the truth 96% of the time, as sommer’s did, but were about 20% narrower (mean width 0.57 against 0.72), with a slightly smaller median error (0.096 against 0.102).
  • Regularisation where the data are thin. The prior keeps poorly identified blocks in range, where REML can stop at a boundary or at an optimum it cannot certify. With few individuals per trait that matters more than it does here.
  • Diagnostics that say when a block is not identified (split-Rhat, divergent transitions), as in the next section.

With many individuals, as here, the REML engines are the quick choice and agree with stanimal; stanimal pays off for smaller samples, or when the uncertainty of G is the point.

Choosing the LKJ prior for stanimal

stanimal puts an LKJ prior on the correlation matrix of each block. With lkj_prior = 1 it is uniform over correlation matrices; larger values pull the block correlations towards zero. indri’s default for its blocks is 1. In simulations of 12 to 35 traits on a few hundred individuals, blocks of up to four traits under LKJ(1) gave 95% intervals that covered the true genetic correlations about 95% of the time and the least shrinkage of strong correlations; LKJ(2) shrank more and covered less (89-94%).

A stronger prior earns its keep when the data cannot identify the block correlations: blocks whose chains disagree (split-Rhat at or above 1.05) or diverge, which indri reports with a warning. On another mouse data set (35 skull traits, 329 mice) a third of the blocks did not mix under LKJ(1), and longer chains did not help; a stronger prior is the remedy there, not more iterations.

The same nine traits under three priors:

A slope below 1 against the REML estimates is the prior’s shrinkage. With 1548 mice the data dominate: from LKJ(1) to LKJ(4) the slope drops only from 0.93 to 0.87 and the intervals barely narrow. As many blocks fail to mix under every prior (six of 33); under LKJ(1) they are:

A stronger prior does not reduce their number, so here the prior is not what keeps them from mixing: those blocks need longer chains, or the REML estimates, which agree with the mixed blocks. With a few hundred individuals, as in the 35-trait data above, the prior matters much more. The rule of thumb:

  1. Start with the default, LKJ(1). It is the least informative choice, and in simulations it gave nominal coverage.
  2. Look at diagnostics. If blocks do not mix (Rhat >= 1.05, divergent transitions), refit with a stronger prior (2, then 4) and see whether they mix and the estimates move. If they mix only under the stronger prior, keep it and report it: the correlations are shrunk towards zero accordingly. If nothing changes, as here, the prior is not the problem: run longer chains, or rely on REML for those pairs.
  3. Compare with a REML engine. sommer takes seconds; a stanimal fit far from it, beyond what the prior’s shrinkage explains, is worth a second look.

G and the phenotypic covariance P point the same way (random skewers 0.91: the two respond alike to the same selection), but G is the more integrated, its correlations concentrated in fewer directions (relative eigenvalue variance 0.049 against 0.015). Residual variation spreads P out over more directions than the genetic variation alone.

Summary

  • The three engines estimate the same G; sommer and asreml agree closely and take seconds, stanimal takes much longer and gives posterior draws.
  • Start stanimal at LKJ(1). Raise the prior for blocks that do not mix only if that makes them mix; with many individuals, as here, it changes little.
  • G here includes variance shared by littermates.
